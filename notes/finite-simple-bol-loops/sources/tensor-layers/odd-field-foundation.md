# Noncentral tensor radicals over a swapped simple square: simplicity test and odd-characteristic exclusion

**Mira Cult**

**Scope.** These concern the original left Bol folder, not a quotient loop or a central extension. Let $`q=p^f`$ be odd, $`d\ge3`$, $`(d,q-1)=1`$, $`T=\mathrm{SL}_d(q)=\mathrm{PSL}_d(q)`$, and suppose the faithful canonical group is an extension

```math
1\longrightarrow V=\operatorname{Mat}_d(q)^+
  \longrightarrow G\longrightarrow T\times T\longrightarrow1,
  \qquad (a,b)X=aXb^{\mathsf t}.                                      \tag{1}
```

Assume canonical inversion induces the ordinary factor swap in these matched natural coordinates. No splitting, prescribed stabilizer, or choice of a marked lift is assumed. Then:
* the negative-transpose action on $`V`$ is impossible for every such $`d,q`$;
* the positive-transpose action is impossible if either projected stabilizer endpoint is full, and for **every** odd $`q`$ at $`d=3`$ satisfying the stated centerless condition, with no endpoint restriction.

In particular (1) gives no finite simple nonassociative left Bol loops in the entire indicated rank-three family. The [rank-capacity argument](tensor-capacity.md) treats higher odd-rank positive transpose with both endpoints proper. These are constraints on a specified faithful irreducible normal layer, not on arbitrary groups containing a matrix subgroup. In characteristic two the two signs coincide and the odd-kernel section argument does not apply.

## The single marked quotient orbit and an intrinsic simplicity test

First more generally let $`N\triangleleft G`$ be inversion-stable with quotient $`T^2`$, swapped as above for any nonabelian simple $`T`$. The projected marked set is the single outside conjugacy class and projected translations are all $`k(x)=(x,x^{-1})`$. The original fibers therefore have a common size $`r`$ by conjugation, whether or not individual lifts form an orbit. With $`H`$ core-free, put $`W=N\cap H,\ J=HN/N,\ \Delta=[N:W]`$. Every $`J`$-cell is covered by exactly $`m=\Delta/r`$ projected labels by original transversality; $`|J|=m|T|`$, and its actual endpoints $`A,B`$ satisfy $`T=BA`$, by evaluating $`T^2=k(T)J`$ at $`(g,1)`$. If (as for a simple loop) there is no quotient by the $`N`$-orbits, then $`m>1`$: equality would give the projected sharp section of size $`|T|`$ on $`[T^2:J]=|T|`$, hence a proper original loop quotient; $`\Delta>1`$ since $`N\ne1`$ is not in the core.

Conversely, for an already valid faithful folder, $`m>1`$ excludes **every** proper original congruence whose normal translation-action kernel contains $`N`$. Indeed if that kernel projects onto both factors then it is $`G`$; otherwise its projection, being normal in $`T^2`$, misses at least one factor. The corresponding section fibers still distinguish the projected label $`x`$ by that factor, so have at most $`r`$ members, whereas each orbit has size at least $`\Delta>r`$. For a genuine quotient these must be equal: the normal kernel of the action on quotient points contains precisely the translations of elements in the identity class, and its orbits are exactly the quotient classes. Thus when $`N`$ is the unique minimal normal subgroup, simplicity is **equivalent** to this single strict inequality. More generally for a minimal $`N`$, any residual proper congruence kernel must commute with $`N`$, since its intersection with $`N`$ is trivial. No control of an arbitrary quotient folder is assumed.

For (1), the matrices of the two natural actions generate the full algebra of endomorphisms over $`\mathbb F_q`$: elementary row and column transvections and their differences give all matrix units with arbitrary field coefficients, and their products act on individual entries. Hence $`V`$ is irreducible even as an additive $`\mathbb F_p`$-module, with centralizer exactly field scalars. The $`T^2`$-action is faithful (the possible multiplicative scalar kernel is absent), so $`C_G(V)=V`$; it is the unique minimal normal subgroup. As transpose implements the factor swap, an intertwiner for canonical inversion on $`V`$ is a scalar times transpose, and squaring to one yields exactly the two signs.

For any outside involution $`\lambda`$ the fiber of outside involutions in its $`V`$-coset is $`\lambda\ker(1+\lambda|_V)`$. Because $`p`$ is odd, this kernel is $`(1-\lambda)V`$, and **every** member is obtained by $`V`$-conjugation. All of it must be selected when $`\lambda`$ is selected. Thus $`r=q^{d_-}`$ for the dimension $`d_-`$ of the negative eigenspace, not $`1`$ and not necessarily $`|V|`$. The differences between two marks over the same projected mark fill precisely that eigenspace. The conjugates of all those spaces must avoid the actual $`W\setminus0`$, since $`H\cap\Lambda^2=1`$. Writing $`d_+=d^2-d_-`$ gives

```math
m=q^{d_+}/|W|>1,\qquad m\mid |T|.                   \tag{2}
```

In particular $`m`$ is a positive power of the defining prime. $`W`$ need only be additive over $`\mathbb F_p`$; no unjustified $`\mathbb F_q`$-dimension bound on it is used.

## Extension and cocycle control, including the small torus

For the natural $`T`$-module $`L=\mathbb F_q^d`$, $`H^0(T,L)=H^1(T,L)=0`$. For $`H^1`$, normalize a derivation to vanish on the determinant-one diagonal torus by averaging over that group of order prime to $`p`$. For a root element $`x_{ij}(c)=1+cE_{ij}`$ its value must be fixed by the diagonal subgroup with $`i`$th and $`j`$th entries equal. This subgroup has no common fixed vector when $`d\ge4`$: to move a coordinate in the pair use a diagonal value different from one on both and balance the product elsewhere; to move an outside coordinate balance on a second outside coordinate. At $`d=3,q>3`$ take $`(a,a,a^{-2})`$ with $`a^2\ne1`$. The root elements generate $`T`$. For $`d=q=3`$ the fixed space is only the third coordinate: write $`\delta(x_{ij}(1))=c_{ij}e_k`$ with $`k\notin\{i,j\}`$. Commuting $`x_{ij}`$ and $`x_{ik}`$ gives $`c_{ij}=c_{ik}=c_i`$. Applying the derivation to $`x_{ij}x_{jk}=x_{jk}x_{ij}x_{ik}`$ gives the extra term $`c_i e_i+2c_i e_j=0`$ on the right, hence $`c_i=0`$ for every row. This proves the assertion including this exception to the simpler torus argument.

In fact **every** group extension in (1) splits, all its complements are $`V`$-conjugate and some complement is $`\tau`$-stable. For completeness $`V=L\boxtimes L`$. Take the tensor product over $`\mathbb F_q`$ of the finite-group bar resolutions for the two factors: termwise these are free over the product group algebra, and exactness follows over the field. Applying $`\operatorname{Hom}(-,L\boxtimes L)`$ gives the tensor of the two cochain complexes by termwise finiteness. Künneth over the field gives $`H^i(T^2,V)=\bigoplus_{a+b=i}H^a(T,L)\otimes H^b(T,L)`$. Both $`i=1,2`$ vanish by the preceding paragraph. These are ordinary group cochains with values in the additive module $`V`$: vanishing in degree two permits changing a section by a cochain to kill its factor set; degree one conjugates all splittings by $`V`$. The set of complements is therefore a single orbit of the odd group $`V`$; the involution $`\tau`$ acting on that odd-size set fixes at least one. In such a complement $`S`$ the action is exactly the quotient swap, so $`k(T)`$ **inside $`S`$** is contained in the actual $`\Lambda^2`$. All subsequent conjugations of $`H`$ preserve the original collision-avoidance test, because $`\Lambda^2`$ is $`G`$-invariant.

If $`A=T`$ (or symmetrically $`B=T`$), Goursat and $`|J|>|T|`$ force $`J=T\times B`$, $`|B|=m`$: a nontrivial common quotient would be $`T`$, forcing a graph of order $`|T|`$. Here $`W`$ is invariant under the full left matrix action. It consists of **all** matrices whose individual rows lie in a fixed $`\mathbb F_q`$-space $`D`$: apply transvection differences and their products to isolate arbitrary field multiples of any row in any position, then add. Hence $`V/W`$ is a sum of copies of the natural module for this $`T`$; its invariants and degree-one cohomology vanish. Relative to the stable complement $`S`$, $`H/W`$ is the graph of a derivation $`J\to V/W`$ in the semidirect product $`(V/W)\rtimes J`$ (only the inverse image of $`J`$ is quotiented here). Conjugate by a lift in $`V`$ so its restriction to the pure $`T`$ vanishes. Commutativity with this $`T`$ forces the remaining derivation values on $`B`$ into the zero fixed space. Thus the conjugated $`H`$ contains the genuine standard $`T\times B`$ in $`S`$; taking $`1\ne b\in B`$ gives $`(b^{-1},b)\in H\cap\Lambda^2`$, impossible. The right-action case uses columns, or interchange of identifications. This proves the full-endpoint exclusion for **both** signs, without needing a classification of $`W`$ by rank.

## Minus transpose, every indicated dimension

The negative eigenspace of $`-X^{\mathsf t}`$ consists of **symmetric** matrices. Every matrix is $`T\times T`$-equivalent to a symmetric one: at less than full rank, row/column reduction can be adjusted to determinant one on null directions on both sides; at full rank use a diagonal matrix of the same determinant and a single determinant-one multiplier. Thus $`W=0`$. Equation (2) gives $`m=q^{d(d-1)/2}=|T|_p`$. Since $`|J|=m|T|`$, both endpoint projections contain full Sylow $`p`$-subgroups. We show directly why two proper such endpoints cannot factor $`T`$.

Any proper subgroup containing the full upper unitriangular $`U`$ stabilizes a nonzero proper subspace. Indeed Gaussian elimination/Bruhat gives $`T=U\,N\,U`$ for the determinant-one monomial group $`N`$ (diagonal factors are absorbed into $`N`$). A subgroup containing $`U`$ is thus generated by $`U`$ and its monomial elements. On the $`d`$ coordinate indices take directed edges $`i\to j`$ for all $`i<j`$ and all their images by the permutation group of those monomials. The subgroup contains all root groups for these edges, and by elementary commutators for every edge in their transitive closure. If the directed graph is strongly connected, all elementary roots occur and the subgroup is $`T`$. Otherwise its strongly connected components are totally ordered, because all forward edges occur, and invariant in that order under the monomials. The span of an initial nonempty proper union is invariant under them and $`U`$. This supplies the asserted actual invariant subspace. A conjugate Sylow gives a conjugate conclusion. Two proper subspace stabilizers cannot factor $`T`$: the stabilizer of one subspace is not transitive on the Grassmannian of the dimension of the other, since the intersection dimension varies. Thus an endpoint is full, already impossible.

## Plus transpose in rank three, including characteristic three

Here $`d_+=6`$, and the negative eigenspace consists of skew-symmetric $`3\times3`$ matrices. Its $`T^2`$-orbit union includes **all** rank-two matrices by determinant-adjusted equivalence at nonfull rank. Thus every nonzero element of $`W`$ has rank one or three. We proved above that both endpoints must be proper. We show their factorization/order conditions imply either an immediate contradiction or $`p=3,\ m\le3`$.

Use the arbitrary-prime-power proper maximal-subgroup list in Nick Gill, [*PSL(3,q) and line-transitive linear spaces*, Theorem 19](https://cage.ugent.be/geometry/Files/148/psl3alltogether.pdf), §3.1, pp.6–7, with its external classification dependencies. It gives point/line parabolic, split triangle, Singer normalizer, subfield, square-field unitary, $`A_6`$, Hessian types, conic, and $`\mathrm{PSL}_2(7)`$; only necessary maximal-order types are used. We do **not** apply the prime/square-only Dieulefait–Vila roster to all extension degrees. By Zsigmondy choose a primitive prime $`\rho`$ of $`p^{3f}-1`$ (no exception for odd $`p`$ and $`3f\ge3`$). Since $`q`$ has order three modulo $`\rho`$, this prime divides $`|T|`$, not $`q(q^2-1)`$. Proper subfields miss it since their maximal defining exponent is $`3f/b<3f`$ for $`b\ge2`$, and their outer multiplier divides three. At least one endpoint contains $`\rho`$; both must contain $`p`$ because $`m>1`$ is a $`p`$-power.

If $`p\ne3`$, the hypothesis $`(3,q-1)=1`$ forces $`p\equiv2\bmod3`$ and $`f`$ odd. There is no unitary subfield. Singer normalizers are $`p`$-free. Exceptional orders have primes at most seven; if $`f>1`$, $`\rho\ge3f+1>7`$. If $`f=1,\rho=7`$, the potentially relevant $`\mathrm{PSL}_2(7)`$ has primes $`2,3,7`$; none is this $`p\ge5,\ p\equiv2\bmod3`$, and $`A_6`$ and Hessian groups have no factor seven. All remaining exceptional possibilities miss $`\rho`$ (the only prime $`\le7`$ of order-three support other than three is seven). No proper subgroup can contain both primes.

Let $`p=3`$. When $`f`$ is odd, $`\rho>7`$: for $`f=1`$ take 13 and for $`f\ge3`$ use $`3f+1`$. Thus the $`\rho`$-endpoint must lie in a Singer normalizer, of order $`3(q^2+q+1)`$. For even $`f`$, put $`q=s^2`$ and also take $`\sigma`$ primitive for $`3^{3f/2}-1`$; $`\sigma`$ is distinct from $`\rho`$, is at least seven, and $`s`$ has order three modulo $`\sigma`$. If the $`\rho`$-endpoint lies in the square-field unitary or a small exceptional group, it misses $`\sigma`$: the unitary order divides $`s^3(s^3+1)(s^2-1)`$, while exceptional orders have at most one distinct prime at least seven. The other proper endpoint must contain $`\sigma`$. Point/line stabilizers, split triangles and conic types miss it as $`q=s^2`$ still has order three; the same unitary misses it. A subfield type needs $`3f/2\le3f/b`$, hence $`b=2`$; its multiplier is trivial here. The Singer normalizer and all small exceptions have order at most $`|\mathrm{SL}_3(s)|`$ for $`s\ge3`$ (compare $`3(s^4+s^2+1)`$, or 360, with $`s^3(s^3-1)(s^2-1)`$). The first endpoint has order at most $`|\mathrm{SU}_3(s)|`$. Even their product is strictly too small:

```math
|\mathrm{SL}_3(s)|\,|\mathrm{SU}_3(s)|/|T|=(s^2-1)/(s^2+1)<1.
```

The same applies to the potentially exceptional $`\rho=7`$ at $`q=9`$. Thus also in even extension degree there must be a Singer-normalizer endpoint. The maximal list gives $`|A|,|B|\le q^3(q-1)^2(q+1)=|T|/(q^2+q+1)`$: compare the displayed types (a subfield/unitary order is $`<2q^4`$, the parabolic is $`>q^5`$; $`q=3`$ already has parabolic order 432 above the small exceptions). Therefore $`m=|J|/|T|\le3`$. This would require $`|W|\ge q^6/3`$.

For $`q\ge9`$ this contradicts an elementary **additive**, not field-linear, bound. To each $`X\in W`$ assign the binary incidence vector of the projective points on its graph in $`\mathbb F_q^3\oplus\mathbb F_q^3`$. The graph has $`1+q+q^2`$ points, an odd number. For distinct $`X,Y`$, the kernel of $`X-Y`$ has dimension zero or two, contributing zero or $`q+1`$ common points, both even. Thus the vectors are orthonormal over $`\mathbb F_2`$ and independent. There are only $`(q^6-1)/(q-1)<q^6/3`$ coordinates. For $`q=3`$ any five-dimensional rank-two-avoiding space is also impossible. All its rank-one matrices, if any, share one image line or one row line: two differing in both directions sum to rank two, and once two have the same image and distinct rows a third with another image cannot share both rows. Transpose if needed and call the resulting rank-one space, together with zero, $`L_0`$; all singular matrices are in it. For every nonzero vector $`v`$, the kernel of evaluation $`W\to\mathbb F_3^3`$ has dimension at least two and lies in $`L_0`$. If $`L_0=0`$ this is already impossible. If $`\dim L_0\le2`$, choose $`v`$ moved by some member of $`L_0`$; its evaluation kernel in $`L_0`$ has dimension at most one. Thus $`\dim L_0=3`$: it contains **all** maps into one line $`\langle c\rangle`$. There is an invertible $`X\in W\setminus L_0`$; take $`\ell(X^{-1}c)=-1`$. The member $`X+c\ell`$ has rank two (an invertible map times an identity plus a rank-one map with one-dimensional kernel), contradiction. Any larger space would contain a five-dimensional one. This excludes $`|W|\ge3^5`$ and completes the proof.

The [stdlib control](../code/verify_swapped_tensor_radical.py) checks all 19,683 rank-three matrices over $`\mathbb F_3`$, actual transvection-closure of both eigenspaces, all 28 full-left submodules, all 729 residual small-torus coefficient tuples, and the order bounds for 12 odd fields including nonsquare $`125,1331`$ and square $`9,81,729`$. The symmetric orbit union is all matrices; the skew union has 8,113 elements. These are author computations, not an independent proof review or a classification beyond (1).
