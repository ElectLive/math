## 1. The classification problem and its answer

A finite simple loop need not have a simple left multiplication group. This paper treats the case in which that group is a direct power of one projective special linear group of degree two. The result gives explicit constructions and an isomorphism classification, rather than a search through multiplication tables.

Write $T=\mathrm{PSL}_2(q)$, where $q\geq4$ is a prime power, and put

```math
|T|=\frac{q(q^2-1)}{\gcd(2,q-1)}.
```

Here a *proper* Bol loop means a Bol loop that is not Moufang. We use left Bol loops throughout. The *canonical left multiplication group* is the faithful permutation group generated by the loop's left translations, not an arbitrarily chosen group containing it.

**Theorem 1 (classification in the PSL₂-power case).** Let $Q$ be a finite simple nonassociative left Bol loop whose entire canonical left multiplication group is $T^n$. Then $n=2k$ for some $k\geq1$. The loop is isomorphic to a path loop $Q(T,A,B,k)$ from Section 2, with endpoint pair from the following table. Conversely, every listed pair and every $k\geq1$ give such a loop. Its order is $|T|^k$; it is proper and is not a Bruck loop.

| Field size | Endpoint subgroups $A,B<T$ | Number of classes for each $k$ |
|---|---|---:|
| $q$ even | $B_q,\ C_{q+1}$ | 1 |
| $q=5$ | $A_4,\ C_5$ | 1 |
| $q=7$ | $7{:}3,\ D_8$; $C_7,\ S_4$ | 2 |
| $q=11$ | $11{:}5,\ D_{12}$; $11{:}5,\ A_4$; $C_{11},\ A_5$ | 3 |
| $q=23$ | $23{:}11,\ D_{24}$; $23{:}11,\ S_4$ | 2 |
| $q=29$ | $29{:}7,\ A_5$ | 1 |
| $q=59$ | $59{:}29,\ D_{60}$; $59{:}29,\ A_5$ | 2 |
| All other odd $q\equiv3\pmod4$ | $B_q,\ D_{q+1}$ | 1 |
| All other odd $q\equiv1\pmod4$ | None | 0 |

The notation $D_m$ denotes a dihedral group **of order $m$**. The Borel subgroup $B_q$ has order $q(q-1)/\gcd(2,q-1)$. A notation such as $29{:}7$ denotes the corresponding semidirect subgroup of the Borel. Each pair means its simultaneous automorphism orbit, with interchange of endpoints allowed. Since $\mathrm{PSL}_2(4)\cong\mathrm{PSL}_2(5)\cong A_5$, these two descriptions give the same family and must not be counted twice.

The theorem concerns an entire multiplication group $T^n$. It does not classify loops whose group merely has PSL₂ composition factors, has socle $T^n$ inside a larger group, has a nontrivial solvable radical, or mixes nonisomorphic simple factors. “Not Bruck” also does not mean “not isotopic to a Bruck loop.”

The proof below gives the construction, its simplicity and its isomorphism criterion, followed by the principal exhaustion argument. Two substantial ingredients are stated explicitly as inputs: the removal of fixed simple factors under canonical inversion, and the exceptional-field subgroup analysis. Their proofs are linked in Section 6. Thus this is a proof assembled from stated results, not a claim that all those arguments are reproduced here.

## 2. The path construction

Let $T$ be any finite nonabelian simple group, and let $T=AB$ be an exact factorization by nontrivial proper subgroups: $A\cap B=1$ and $|A||B|=|T|$.

Take a path with $k$ edges. Put a copy of $T$ on each edge and identify their product with $N=T^k$. Put $A$ at one endpoint, $B$ at the other, and a full copy of $T$ at each internal vertex. Embed each vertex group diagonally into its incident edge coordinates. Two-color the path; let $L$ and $R$ be the products of the vertex groups of the two respective colors. Groups of the same color have disjoint supports.

An element of $L\cap R$ must have the same value on every edge, by propagation along the path. That value lies in $A\cap B$, so $L\cap R=1$. Moreover,

```math
|L||R|=|T|^{k-1}|A||B|=|T|^k=|N|.
```

Consequently $N=LR$ is an exact factorization. In $G=N\times N$, define

```math
H=L\times R,
\qquad K=\{(x,x^{-1}):x\in N\}.
```

The associated loop is denoted $Q(T,A,B,k)$.

**Proposition 2 (existence and simplicity).** This datum is a faithful generated left Bol folder. It defines a finite simple proper non-Bruck loop of order $|T|^k$, with canonical left multiplication group $T^{2k}$.

**Proof.** We use the faithful-folder and normal-quotient criteria stated in the foundational results cited in Section 6. Here we verify their concrete hypotheses.

Let $\tau$ exchange the two copies of $N$. The set $K$ is inverse-closed and closed under $xyx$. Its product set is

```math
K^2=\{(u,v):v\text{ is conjugate in }N\text{ to }u^{-1}\}.
```

An exact factorization $N=LR$ satisfies $L\cap R^g=1$ for every $g\in N$. Indeed, write $g=r\ell$ with $r\in R$, $\ell\in L$, and conjugate the intersection by $\ell$. Thus $H\cap K^2=1$. Together with $|K|=[G:H]=|N|$, this makes $K$ a transversal to $H$ and its conjugates. Since $N$ is perfect, the anti-diagonal set $K$ generates $N\times N$: its products generate the commutator subgroups in each coordinate as well as the anti-diagonal elements.

Every normal subgroup of $G$ is a product of its simple coordinate factors. No such factor is contained in $H$: an endpoint group is proper, and an internal vertex group is diagonal on two coordinates. Hence $H$ is core-free.

It remains to prove simplicity. A quotient of $G$ retains coordinate sets $I,J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}$ in its two copies of $N$. Let $\pi$ be the quotient map. A vertex is active if any of its incident half-edges is retained. On active vertices retain an edge only when both half-edges are retained. This graph is a forest. Write $c$ for its number of components, $i$ for its active internal vertices, and $e_A,e_B\in\{0,1\}$ for the activity of the endpoints. Then

```math
|\pi K|=|T|^{|I\cup J|},
\qquad |\pi H|=|T|^i|A|^{e_A}|B|^{e_B},
\qquad |I\cap J|=i+e_A+e_B-c.
```

Therefore

```math
\frac{|\pi K|}{[\pi G:\pi H]}
=|T|^{c-e_A-e_B}|A|^{e_A}|B|^{e_B}.                 \tag{1}
```

For a nonempty retained set, (1) exceeds one if neither endpoint is active or if exactly one is active. If both are active, it equals $|T|^{c-1}$. Equality then requires $c=1$; a connected subgraph of the path containing both endpoints contains every edge, with both half-edges retained. This is the identity quotient. The empty retained set gives the trivial quotient. Every other quotient has strict inequality in (1), so the exact normal-quotient criterion proves simplicity.

The group $H$ is nontrivial, so the loop is nonassociative. There is a strict intermediate subgroup

```math
H=L\times R\ <\ L\times N\ <\ G.
```

The canonical action is therefore imprimitive. The foundational Moufang primitivity result excludes a simple nonassociative Moufang loop here. Finally, $\tau(H)=R\times L\ne H$; the canonical Bruck-stabilizer criterion excludes a Bruck loop. This proves the proposition. $\square$

## 3. There is no hidden duplication within the path family

**Proposition 3 (isomorphism criterion).** Two path loops $Q(T,A,B,k)$ and $Q(T',A',B',k')$ are isomorphic exactly when $k=k'$ and there is an isomorphism $\psi:T\to T'$ carrying $(A,B)$ to $(A',B')$ or to $(B',A')$.

**Proof.** Such an isomorphism acts on every edge coordinate; reversing the path handles interchange of endpoints. It preserves $H$ and $K$ and hence gives a loop isomorphism.

Conversely, a loop isomorphism induces a canonical-folder isomorphism. It commutes with canonical inversion, which is uniquely determined on the generating set $K$. The simple direct factors of $G$ therefore identify $T$ with $T'$ and give $k=k'$. Commutation with the swap allows a permutation of paired coordinates, a swap within each pair, and an automorphism $\psi_i$ of $T$ applied to both members of each pair.

For $k\geq2$, the internal vertex groups are precisely the minimal normal subgroups of $H$ isomorphic to $T$. The proper endpoint groups cannot contain a copy of $T$. Supports of these intrinsic internal groups, together with the coordinate pairing, recover the path. An isomorphism can only preserve or reverse it. Preservation of each internal diagonal forces $\psi_i=\psi_{i+1}$, so connectedness gives one common automorphism $\psi$. Its action on the endpoint groups gives the stated criterion. For $k=1$, the same conclusion follows directly from the centralizer of the swap in the automorphism group of $T\times T$. $\square$

## 4. Why the exhaustion argument produces paths

The construction above works for arbitrary simple $T$, but it does not by itself show that all relevant loops arise this way. The additional restrictions on proper subgroups of PSL₂ are what make an exhaustion possible.

First take the *paired case*: canonical inversion pairs all simple factors. After choosing coordinates,

```math
G=T^k\times T^k,
\qquad K=\{(x,x^{-1}):x\in T^k\}.
```

The canonical section is the full anti-diagonal, $|H|=|T|^k$, and $H\cap K^2=1$.

The strip-forest reduction gives the following description. Full coordinate projections of $H$ assemble into disjoint twisted diagonal copies of $T$, called *strips*. The remaining coordinates have proper projections $A_1,\ldots,A_\ell<T$. They form a residual subgroup $R\leq\prod_i A_i$, which is not initially assumed to be a direct product. Make each strip a vertex, each proper coordinate a leaf, and each inversion pair an edge. The resulting graph is a forest. If it has $c$ components, then

```math
|R|=|T|^{\ell-c},\qquad \ell\geq2c.                 \tag{2}
```

For a proper nonempty union $C$ of components, with $\ell_C$ leaves and $c_C$ components, the simplicity criterion further gives

```math
|R_C|>|T|^{\ell_C-c_C},                            \tag{3}
```

where $R_C$ is the residual projection. These are consequences of simplicity, not assumptions that the residual subgroup splits. This distinction matters: connectedness and independence will follow from order saturation.

**Lemma 4 (two-prime saturation).** Suppose distinct primes $p,r$ divide $|T|$, with valuations $a,b>0$. Assume every proper subgroup of $T$ has $p$-valuation at most $a$ and $r$-valuation at most $b$, and no proper subgroup has order divisible by both primes. In (2), at least $\ell-c$ leaf projections have positive $p$-valuation, and at least $\ell-c$ have positive $r$-valuation. Hence $\ell=2c$, every component is a path, and each nonzero valuation attains its maximum.

**Proof.** Projection embeds $R$ into the product of its leaf projections. Its $p$-valuation is $a(\ell-c)$, and each leaf contributes at most $a$. Thus at least $\ell-c$ leaves contribute. The same argument applies to $r$. The two sets of leaves are disjoint, so $\ell\geq2(\ell-c)$, or $\ell\leq2c$. Equality follows from (2). A finite tree with exactly two leaves is a path. Equality in the valuation bounds gives saturation at every contributing leaf. $\square$

For the generic odd-field case, $p$ is the characteristic and $r$ is a suitable prime dividing $q+1$. The PSL₂ subgroup theorem makes the full-characteristic leaves lie in Borels $B_q$, while the other leaves lie in nonsplit torus normalizers $D_{q+1}$. Since $|B_q||D_{q+1}|=|T|$, equality in

```math
|T|^c=|R|\leq\prod_i|A_i|
\leq \bigl(|B_q||D_{q+1}|\bigr)^c=|T|^c             \tag{4}
```

forces the leaf groups to be the full displayed subgroups and forces $R=\prod_i A_i$.

A path with two dihedral endpoints violates $H\cap K^2=1$: choose an involution at each endpoint and propagate involutions along its strips. The involutions of odd-characteristic PSL₂ belong to one class, so the paired coordinates satisfy the conjugacy condition defining $K^2$. There are equally many Borel and dihedral endpoints, so a path with two Borel endpoints would force a second path with two dihedral endpoints. Thus every path has one of each type.

If there were more than one component, its residual projection would have order $|T|$, contrary to the strict inequality (3) for that component. The forest is therefore a single path. Twisted incidence automorphisms can be removed successively along a tree; no cycle imposes a compatibility condition.

When $q\equiv1\pmod4$, both endpoint groups are even, so the same involution collision excludes the path. When $q\equiv3\pmod4$, their orders are coprime and their product is $|T|$, giving an exact factorization. The nonsplit normalizer acts regularly on the projective line; consequently the Borel/normalizer pairs form one simultaneous conjugacy class. Proposition 3 then gives one loop class at each length.

## 5. The field analysis and the fixed-factor reduction

The arithmetic purpose of a primitive divisor is to distinguish the two kinds of leaves in Lemma 4. For $q=p^f$, choose $r$ with multiplicative order $2f$ for $p$ modulo $r$. Then $r\mid q+1$, but $r$ does not divide the orders of proper subfield groups. If also $r>5$, it excludes the exceptional subgroups $A_4,S_4,A_5$. The remaining $r$-bearing proper subgroups lie in a nonsplit normalizer and are characteristic-free. Full characteristic valuation forces a Borel. This supplies the hypotheses used in Section 4.

The complete field analysis is more than this generic observation. Its precise additional conclusions are as follows.

**Lemma 5 (paired PSL₂ exhaustion).** For every prime power $q\geq4$, every simple nonassociative canonical folder with paired group $\mathrm{PSL}_2(q)^{2k}$ is a path folder. Its endpoint-pair orbits are exactly those in Theorem 1.

For odd fields this includes the primitive-divisor argument, prime fields with small torus divisors, square-field valuation exceptions, and the exceptional endpoint groups at $q=5,7,11,23,29,59$. The exceptional alternatives are not obtained merely by comparing orders: admissibility and simultaneous automorphism orbits must also be checked. The even-field argument instead gives Borel/cyclic-torus endpoints. The field $q=9$ contributes no loops. Proofs and computational evidence for the exceptional fusion checks are in the sources below.

**Lemma 6 (no fixed simple factor).** Let $Q$ satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem 1, without assuming a pairing for canonical inversion. That inversion fixes no simple direct factor of its canonical left multiplication group.

In particular it partitions the factors into exchanged pairs, so $n$ is even. This lemma is essential: the paired classification alone does not prove Theorem 1. Its proof uses separate fixed-factor obstructions, including character-theoretic and involution-support arguments for the field cases not settled by the paired analysis.

**Proof of Theorem 1, using Lemmas 5 and 6.** Lemma 6 identifies the group and inversion with the paired situation. Lemma 5 reduces the stabilizer to one path and supplies the listed endpoint pairs. Proposition 2 constructs all those paths and proves simplicity, properness and the claimed multiplication group. Proposition 3 identifies precisely when they are isomorphic. The exceptional orbit checks in Lemma 5 give the counts in the table. $\square$

## 7. Beyond the theorem

The result allows arbitrarily many simple direct factors; its parameter is path length, not a bounded enumeration of small envelopes. It leaves several distinct classification problems:

- Which other finite nonabelian simple groups admit an equally complete description of simple Bol folders over their direct powers?
- What changes when the canonical group is a proper extension of a PSL₂-power socle, rather than the direct power itself?
- Which mixed simple-factor groups admit non-path stabilizers, and what replaces the two-prime saturation argument there?
- Which of the path loops have Bruck isotopes? The non-Bruck conclusion in Theorem 1 does not answer that question.

These questions concern new structural cases, not omissions from the PSL₂-power scope of Theorem 1.
